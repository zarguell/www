# JPEG Artifacts as Texture

We used to hate them. Those blocky, pixelated artifacts that appeared when we saved images at too low a quality setting. JPEG compression was the enemy of clean design.

But what if we've been wrong?

## The Aesthetic of Loss

JPEG artifacts are the visible signature of digital compression. They're what happens when you squeeze visual data into smaller spaces, when you prioritize file size over pixel perfection. They're evidence of *constraints* — and constraints often create interesting aesthetics.

Consider glitch art, vaporwave, and the broader "lo-fi digital" movement. These aesthetics celebrate the imperfections that early digital technologies introduced. Scanlines, dithering, color banding — these aren't bugs, they're features.

## Intentional Artifacts

I've been experimenting with adding JPEG-like artifacts to my designs intentionally. Subtle noise, color quantization, even visible blockiness in the right places. The results have been surprising:

- **Texture**: Artifacts add surface interest that flat design lacks
- **Warmth**: There's something human about imperfection
- **Nostalgia**: Immediate association with early web aesthetics
- **Character**: Every artifact is unique, a fingerprint of compression

## Tools of the Trade

Want to experiment? Try:

- **Exporting images at lower quality** (60-80% in Photoshop)
- **Using CSS filters** to simulate dithering and noise
- **Applying SVG filters** for custom artifact effects
- **Intentionally upscaling and downscaling** images

The key is subtlety. This isn't about making your site look broken — it's about adding a layer of digital patina.

## The Future Is Lo-Fi

As our screens get sharper and our bandwidth faster, there's a counter-movement embracing the lo-fi, the artifacted, the intentionally degraded. Maybe it's a reaction against sterility. Maybe it's nostalgia. Or maybe — just maybe — we're learning that perfection isn't the only goal.

Embrace the artifacts. They tell a story about digital media itself.
